Moving Out of the GTA

Leaving the city: what actually changes when you move out of the GTA

September 2026  ·  8 min read

Guest post by Binny's Tote Rental, Barrie, ON

Does it actually cost less to leave the GTA?

Usually yes, and one of the largest savings has nothing to do with the purchase price.

Toronto is the only municipality in Ontario that charges its own land transfer tax. Buy inside Toronto's city limits and you pay both the provincial Land Transfer Tax and the Toronto Municipal Land Transfer Tax, which broadly mirrors it. Buy anywhere else in the province and you pay the provincial portion only.

On an $800,000 home, that's roughly $12,475 in provincial LTT outside Toronto, versus about $24,950 combined inside it. On a $1,000,000 home, roughly $16,475 versus $32,950.

That's a five-figure difference payable in cash on closing day. It can't be rolled into your mortgage.

Watch out: this applies to leaving Toronto proper, not the GTA generally. Mississauga, Brampton, Vaughan, Markham and the rest of the 905 already pay provincial LTT only. If you're moving from Brampton to Barrie, the land transfer tax picture doesn't change much. If you're moving from Etobicoke, it changes a lot.

What else moves on the money side

Property tax rates are usually higher outside Toronto, which surprises people. Toronto has one of the lowest residential property tax rates in the province. It can afford to, because assessed values are so high. Smaller municipalities often have higher rates on lower-valued homes. Your actual bill usually still drops, but not proportionally to the price difference.

Some costs appear that you didn't have before. Snow clearing. Septic pump-outs. Well maintenance and testing. A second vehicle, in many cases. Longer drives to everything.

And some disappear. Parking. Transit passes for a whole household. The premium on nearly everything a dense city charges for.

How bad is the commute, really?

This is the question that decides most moves, and the honest answer is: worse than the map suggests, and it's the thing most likely to make people regret leaving.

If you're commuting to downtown Toronto from north of the GTA, plan on around 1 hour 45 minutes each way by GO train from somewhere like Barrie. That's roughly three and a half hours of your day.

Driving is nominally faster off-peak and considerably worse on-peak. Highway 400 southbound in the morning and northbound in the evening is the standard pattern. Summer Friday afternoons northbound are their own phenomenon: cottage traffic turns a one-hour drive into something else entirely.

Three things worth being honest with yourself about:

  1. Do the commute before you commit. Not once, on a Sunday. On a Tuesday in February at the actual hour you'd be travelling.
  2. Proximity to a station matters enormously. A ten-minute walk to a GO station is a completely different life from a twenty-minute drive plus parking.
  3. The people who thrive are the ones who use the time. Readers, sleepers, people who genuinely work on the train. If you can't, three and a half hours a day is a large thing to give up.

Tip: do the commute before you commit, on a weekday, in winter, at the hour you'd actually be travelling. A Sunday test drive tells you nothing.

The people happiest with the move are usually those who don't commute daily: remote workers, people who found work locally, or households where one person commutes and the other doesn't.

What changes about the house itself?

This is the section people skip and then learn the hard way. Outside serviced urban areas, you become the utility company for several things you never thought about.

Well water

Many properties outside town boundaries are on private wells. If yours is one, you, not a municipality, are responsible for the safety of your drinking water.

  • Testing is free. Public Health Ontario laboratories test private drinking water for E. coli and total coliform bacteria at no charge. Sample bottles are available through your local public health unit.
  • Test three to four times a year once you've established a clean baseline, and always after flooding, repairs, or any change in taste, smell or appearance.
  • Water that looks and tastes completely normal can still be contaminated. Testing is the only way to know.
  • Samples must reach the lab within 48 hours and typically aren't accepted on Fridays. Collect just before drop-off.
  • The free test covers bacteria only, not chemicals or minerals. Those need a private licensed lab.

Septic systems

If there's no municipal sewer, there's a septic tank and a weeping bed somewhere on your property.

  • Find out where both are and keep a sketch with your house documents.
  • Pump the tank out on a schedule. Solids accumulate and have to be removed periodically.
  • Slow drains or backing-up toilets are a warning, potentially that the weeping bed is failing. Don't wait it out. Replacement is one of the most expensive repairs in rural homeownership.
  • Nothing but wastewater goes in. No grease, no chemicals, no "flushable" wipes, no coffee grounds.
  • Don't park or build on the weeping bed.
  • Watch your water volume. Six loads of laundry in an afternoon can overwhelm a system that handles them fine across a week.

Utilities get fragmented

In the city, utilities are simple. Outside it, they're split across more providers, and sometimes the provider depends on your exact address rather than your town. In Barrie, for example, electricity is supplied by either Alectra Utilities or InnPower depending on where the property sits. Not the neighbourhood. The address.

Check with your specific address rather than asking a neighbour.

Internet is a real constraint

Check availability before you commit to a property, especially anywhere rural. Options thin out quickly outside town boundaries, and fibre availability can vary street by street.

If you work from home (which is often the entire reason the move is possible), this is not a detail. It's a condition of the move working at all.

Whatever you arrange, order it four weeks out. Installation routinely runs two to four weeks, worse at month-end.

Winter becomes your job

If you're moving north or west of the GTA, winter is a genuine step up in seriousness, particularly anywhere catching lake effect snow, where cold air crossing warm lake water dumps snow inland. One area can be buried while somewhere twenty minutes away sees almost nothing.

Tip: book snow tires in October and a plow contractor in early autumn. Both fill up before the first storm, and everyone who waits spends three weeks on a waiting list.

Four things that stop being someone else's problem:

  1. Snow tires, not all-seasons. Book the appointment in October. Everyone waits for the first storm and then nobody can get in for three weeks.
  2. Book a plow contractor in early autumn. The good ones fill their routes by October.
  3. Learn your municipality's overnight winter parking rules. Many prohibit on-street parking overnight through the winter months so plows can work. Barrie's ban runs 12:01 a.m. to 7 a.m., December through March. This catches new residents every single year.
  4. Clearing your sidewalk is usually your legal responsibility, within a set window after snowfall.

And the etiquette: the plow will bury the end of your driveway every time. Clear that windrow last, not first, or you'll do it twice.

What about services and daily life?

Healthcare is the one to plan for. Register with Ontario's Health Care Connect early if you don't have a family doctor. Waits for a new patient spot are real in many communities. Know where the nearest emergency department is before you need it.

Waste collection often works differently. Schedules in many municipalities depend on your specific address rather than your street, garbage and organics frequently run on different cycles, and in much of Ontario curbside recycling is now managed by a separate producer organisation rather than the municipality, on a different schedule and a different website from your garbage.

Transit will not be what you're used to. Most communities outside the GTA are driving communities. A household without a car will find life genuinely limited.

The trade (and this is what people who love the move talk about) is space, a yard, a garage, quiet, and being twenty minutes from things that used to be a weekend trip.

Getting your things there

Two practical realities about moving out of the GTA that differ from moving within it.

One: long-distance moves cost more and need more lead time. Movers charge differently once you're outside their standard radius, and good ones book weeks ahead. Month-end dates go first.

Two: reusable tote rental is a local-delivery service on both ends. Totes get dropped off where you pack and collected where you unpack. The provider you want is generally the one that serves your destination, since that's where the empties need collecting after you've unpacked.

Your checklist before you commit

Run through this before you sign anything. Every item is something people wish they'd checked first.

  • Drive the actual commute, on a weekday, at the hour you'd travel
  • Confirm internet availability at the specific address, not the town
  • Read the well water test results and check when they were last done
  • Locate the septic tank and weeping bed, and ask when it was last pumped
  • Confirm which hydro provider serves that exact address
  • Check the municipality's winter parking and sidewalk-clearing rules
  • Ask what a high-water spring actually looks like on that street
  • Work out your land transfer tax and whether you're leaving Toronto proper
  • Budget for snow clearing, well testing and septic pump-outs as ongoing costs
  • Register with Health Care Connect if you don't have a family doctor
  • Book movers and totes early. Month-end dates go first.

Frequently asked questions

Is it cheaper to buy outside Toronto?

Generally yes, and not only on price. Toronto is the only Ontario municipality charging a municipal land transfer tax on top of the provincial one, so buying outside city limits can save roughly $12,000–$16,000 on a typical purchase, payable in cash at closing.

How long is the GO train commute from Barrie to Toronto?

Roughly 1 hour 45 minutes each way to Union Station, from either Allandale Waterfront or Barrie South.

Do I need to test well water, and does it cost anything?

Yes, and no. Public Health Ontario tests private well water for E. coli and total coliform free of charge. Test three to four times a year, and always after flooding or repairs. Samples must reach the lab within 48 hours.

What is the most common thing people underestimate?

The commute, followed by winter maintenance. Both are manageable, but both are significantly more demanding than they look from a listing photo.

Is property tax lower outside the GTA?

The rate is often higher outside Toronto, because Toronto's high assessed values allow a low rate. Your actual bill usually still drops, but by less than the difference in home price would suggest.

The honest summary

Leaving the GTA generally means: more house, more space, a large one-time saving on land transfer tax if you're leaving Toronto proper, and a genuine improvement in what's within twenty minutes of your door.

It also means: a harder commute if you still need one, several utilities you're now personally responsible for, and a winter that expects more of you.

The people who are happiest with it did three things before committing: they drove the actual commute at the actual hour, they checked internet availability at the actual address, and they read the well and septic reports properly instead of skimming them.

Do those three, and the rest is just moving.
